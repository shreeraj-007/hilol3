The question

Is this place really changing, and by how much?

Earth is always changing. When a measured quantity keeps moving in one direction (warmer, wetter, windier) we call that a trend. But the same variable can trend up in one region and down in another, even when the same process drives both. And real trends hide behind huge natural swings, like summer and winter.

What is changing?

Nine variables, from air temperature and rainfall to soil wetness, cloud cover, humidity, wind and air pressure. Each one comes with a plain-language definition and a note on how NASA measures it.

Where is it changing?

We chose record-breaking "benchmark" places, such as Death Valley, the Dead Sea, Mount Everest and the wettest place on Earth, so each variable is studied where it is most extreme.

How much, and is it significant?

For every place we measure the rate of change per year and run a statistical test that asks whether a trend that size could easily have appeared by chance.

How it works

From raw data to a verdict in four steps

Every chart in the explorer goes through the same analysis, written in Python with pandas and SciPy.

  1. 1

    Collect

    One monthly time series per location, January 2006 to January 2026, downloaded as CSV.

  2. 2

    Clean

    Months flagged as missing (−9999) are removed. Up to two missing months in a row are filled in from their neighbours; any still missing are dropped.

  3. 3

    Remove the seasons

    We subtract the long-term average for each calendar month. What is left, the anomaly, shows whether a month was above or below normal for that time of year.

  4. 4

    Test the trend

    A straight line is fitted through the anomalies (linear regression). Its slope is the rate of change; a t-test on the slope gives a p-value, and p < 0.05 counts as significant.

Detective's glossary

The words you'll see in the explorer

Trend
A steady, long-term change in one direction, such as rising or falling, that continues underneath the short-term ups and downs.
Anomaly
How far a month's value is above (+) or below (−) the usual value for that calendar month. Using anomalies removes the regular summer–winter cycle so a trend isn't hidden by the seasons.
Rate of change
The slope of the best-fit straight line: how much the variable changes per year on average, in the variable's own units (for example kelvin per year).
Linear regression
A method that draws the single straight line that fits the data points as closely as possible. The dashed orange line in each chart.
p-value
If there were really no trend, how likely is it that random month-to-month noise would produce a slope at least this steep? A small p-value means "unlikely to be chance alone."
Statistically significant
We call a trend significant when p < 0.05, meaning less than a 5% chance that noise alone would produce it. Significant does not mean large or important. Not significant does not prove there is no change.
95% confidence interval
A range of plausible values for the true rate of change. If the whole range is above zero (or below zero), the direction of the trend is clear.
R² (R-squared)
The share of the month-to-month variation that the straight line explains, from 0 to 1. A trend can be significant even when R² is small, because over 240 months even a gentle slope adds up.

Read the fine print

What these results can and cannot tell us

They can tell us

  • Which direction each variable has moved at each place since 2006.
  • Roughly how fast, in real units per year.
  • Whether a straight-line trend stands out from the month-to-month noise.
  • That one variable can move in opposite directions in different regions.

They cannot tell us

  • Why something changed. A trend is a clue, not a cause.
  • What will happen next. Twenty years is short in climate terms, and trends can speed up, slow down or reverse.
  • About cycles or curved changes. We only test for straight-line trends.
  • Exactly what a ground station would read. Each series comes from satellite and model data for a location, which can differ from local measurements.

Things to keep in mind

  • Neighbouring months tend to resemble each other. The standard test assumes they don't, so p-values close to 0.05 should be treated with extra caution.
  • Some records have gaps. The explorer shows how many months were missing for each place.
  • A non-significant trend can become significant once a longer record is available.
